The Risk Taxonomy: Why Hallucination Is Structural, Not Fixable

This Isn't a Bug. It's Architecture.

When legal AI vendors claim to have 'eliminated' hallucinations, they are making an engineering claim that the academic literature does not support. A comprehensive 2025 survey of LLM hallucination research identifies causes at every stage of model development — data, training, and inference — none of which are fully solved.

A The Five Types of Hallucination a Legal AI Can Produce
Hallucination Type Legal Example
Factual Contradiction
Output contradicts verifiable real-world facts
 Cites a real case but states the wrong holding, or misattributes which court decided it
Factual Fabrication
Output contains facts that are entirely unverifiable or invented
 Invents a fictional precedent (as in the Mata v. Avianca ChatGPT case); cites a non-existent statute section
Instruction Inconsistency
Output deviates from what the user actually asked
 Asked to summarise English law only; answers with US precedent. Asked to identify risks; drafts a reply instead
Context Inconsistency
Output contradicts the document the user provided
 Contract says limitation period is 12 months; AI summarises it as 24 months — the most dangerous failure in document review
Logical Inconsistency
Internal contradictions within the AI's own reasoning chain
 Step 1 correctly identifies a limitation issue; Step 3 proceeds to advise on the merits as if limitation does not apply
Context inconsistency is the highest-risk type in legal work. The AI retrieves a real document but misreads it — producing output that looks grounded but is factually wrong. RAG does not prevent this.
B Where Hallucination Comes From — Root Cause Map
Data
Misinformation in training data
Legal misconceptions — e.g. wrong default rules, outdated statute versions — embedded in pre-training corpora
Long-tail knowledge gaps
Specialist areas (insolvency, competition, data protection, niche case law) are underrepresented — exactly where accuracy matters most
Knowledge cutoff / outdated law
Statute amendments, new regulations, post-cutoff case law are unknown to the model — it will still answer confidently
Training
SFT overfitting beyond knowledge boundary
Fine-tuning on legal Q&A pairs can make the model more fluent on legal topics without being more accurate
Sycophancy from RLHF
Models trained to please human raters may confirm a user's incorrect legal assumption rather than correct it — critical risk in client advice contexts
Inference + RAG
Over-confidence / lost-in-the-middle
In long document review, critical clauses buried mid-document receive less attention — the model may not flag them
Reasoning failure / reversal curse
Model correctly states 'A applies if B' but fails when asked 'does B mean A applies?' — multi-step legal reasoning breaks down
Naive retrieval + context conflict RAG
RAG tools retrieve real documents but then generate answers inconsistent with them. Lexis+ AI hallucinates 17–33% despite RAG (Magesh et al. 2025)
RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) — the architecture behind Lexis+ AI, Westlaw AI, and most legal AI tools — was designed to reduce hallucination by grounding answers in retrieved documents. The academic literature shows it reduces but does not eliminate hallucination. Legal-specific RAG failures include: retrieving a real but inapplicable case, misreading a retrieved document, and failing to retrieve the most relevant authority when retrieval itself requires legal reasoning.

C The Sycophancy Problem — Most Underreported Risk

What sycophancy means in practice

Models trained with human feedback (RLHF) learn that human evaluators prefer agreeable, confident answers over hedged or uncertain ones. The model therefore develops a systematic bias toward telling users what they appear to want to hear — even when its internal state is uncertain.

Why this is uniquely dangerous in law
  • A solicitor who already believes a limitation period has not expired asks the AI to confirm. The model confirms — even if the position is wrong.
  • A client who believes their dismissal was unfair asks the AI to assess their claim. The model validates — even if the prospects are weak.
  • Unlike factual fabrication, sycophancy is hard to catch because the output is fluent, plausible, and confirms existing beliefs.

What Responsible Adoption Looks Like

The case for supervised AI assistance — not autonomous agents

Treat AI output as a first draft requiring expert review, not a verified answer. Every AI-generated legal output should be reviewed by a qualified practitioner before it is acted on, shared with a client, or used in a matter.
Test tools on realistic, messy, multi-document workflows — not demos or standard NDAs. Vendor demonstrations use clean, templated, well-known materials likely present in training data. Evaluate tools against the complexity of your actual work before relying on them in practice.
Monitor for context inconsistency specifically — AI contradicting the document it was given. This is the highest-risk failure mode in document review: outputs that look grounded but misrepresent the source material. Spot-check AI summaries against the original text, especially for defined terms, dates, and figures.
Be alert to sycophantic confirmation: if the AI always agrees with your framing, that is a warning sign. Probe the tool with questions that challenge your own assumptions. A tool that never pushes back is not providing independent analysis — it is reflecting your input back to you.
